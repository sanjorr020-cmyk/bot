# Job Descriptions

Write job descriptions that attract the right candidates and filter out the wrong ones — in that order. A good JD is a sales document with a scoping document underneath it: before any prose, the role's outcomes, level, and real requirements must be pinned down, because most bad JDs fail at scoping, not writing. This skill covers the full path from role definition to publish-ready variants for each posting channel.

## When to use this skill

Use this skill when the user wants to:
- Write a job description for a new or existing role
- Rewrite an underperforming posting (low application volume or wrong-fit applicants)
- Audit a JD for biased language, qualification inflation, or legal risk
- Add or structure compensation information in a posting
- Produce channel variants (careers page, LinkedIn, Indeed, niche boards)

Do NOT use this skill for:
- Interview questions, scorecards, or hiring rubrics — use **interview-kits** (the JD's must-haves feed directly into it; hand off there once the JD is final)
- New-hire ramp plans and first-week logistics — use **employee-onboarding**
- Recruiter outreach messages to passive candidates — use **linkedin-outreach** or **cold-email-outreach** (a JD is pull; outreach is push)
- Employer-brand positioning and company voice — use **brand-messaging** (borrow its output for the "About us" section)
- Promoting the opening on social channels — use **linkedin-content** or **social-content-calendar**

## Inputs to gather

Ask the user for these before drafting. Where they don't know, apply the default and flag the assumption in your output.

| Input | Why it matters | Default if unknown |
|---|---|---|
| Job title (internal + external) | Searchability drives 80%+ of job-board discovery | Propose a standard, searchable title (see Process step 1) |
| Level/seniority | Sets scope of outcomes and comp band | Infer from responsibilities; label as "mid-level" if ambiguous |
| Top 3 outcomes for the first year | The backbone of the whole JD | Derive from the problem the hire solves; confirm with user |
| Manager and team size/structure | Candidates ask; ambiguity reads as chaos | "Reports to [function] lead" placeholder, flagged for user |
| Salary range + key benefits | Postings with pay ranges get more and better applicants; legally required in many jurisdictions | Ask twice; if refused, note the legal-risk caveat and write "competitive" as last resort |
| Location/remote policy | Hard filter for most candidates | Ask; never guess. If truly unknown, write "Remote ([country/timezone] hours)" flagged |
| Employment type | Full-time vs contract changes everything | Full-time |
| 3-5 real deal-breaker requirements | Prevents qualification inflation | Extract from outcomes, then confirm |
| Existing JD or a JD they admire | Voice and structure calibration | Use the templates below |
| Target channels | Determines variants needed | Careers page + LinkedIn + Indeed |

If the user says "just write it," ask ONLY the three highest-leverage questions: (1) What must this person accomplish in the first 12 months? (2) Salary range? (3) Location/remote policy? Then draft with stated assumptions.

## Process

1. **Scope the role before writing a word.** Answer, with the user: Why does this role exist now? What breaks if it stays unfilled for 6 months? Is this one role or two stapled together? (If the outcomes span two distinct functions — e.g., "own demand gen" AND "manage the sales team" — flag it; hybrid roles at non-executive levels get weak applicant pools.) Fix the title: use the term candidates search, not internal branding. "Marketing Manager" outperforms "Growth Wizard"; "Software Engineer, Backend" outperforms "Code Samurai." Keep titles under 60 characters, lead with the function, and avoid internal level codes ("SE II" means nothing externally — write "Software Engineer (Mid-Level)").

2. **Define outcomes, then derive everything else.** Write 3-5 outcome statements for the first 12 months, each measurable or at least verifiable: "Reduce average support first-response time from 8 hours to under 2" beats "handle support tickets." Structure as 90-day / 6-month / 12-month milestones when the user can supply them. Every responsibility bullet and every requirement must trace back to one of these outcomes — if it doesn't, cut it.

3. **Draft responsibilities as outcomes, not activities.** 5-8 bullets, each starting with a strong verb, each answering "to what end?" Formula: **[Verb] + [what] + [measurable why]**. "Write blog posts" → "Publish 4-6 SEO-driven articles per month that grow organic signups." Cap bullets at ~20 words. If you have more than 8 bullets, the role is unscoped — go back to step 1.

4. **Prune requirements ruthlessly.** Build two lists: **Must-haves (4-7 items)** — things a candidate cannot succeed without on day one — and **Nice-to-haves (3-5 items, explicitly labeled)**. Apply the inflation tests to every must-have:
   - *The proxy test:* Is "bachelor's degree" or "X years of experience" standing in for a skill you could name directly? Name the skill instead. Only keep a degree requirement if legally or genuinely necessary (e.g., licensed professions).
   - *The incumbent test:* Would your best current performer in this role have qualified when hired? If not, the bar is fictional.
   - *The years test:* Years-of-experience minimums above 5 for non-leadership roles are almost always inflation. Also check for impossible asks (e.g., more years with a technology than it has existed).
   - *The 60/100 rule:* Research (widely cited from an internal Hewlett-Packard report) shows men apply when they meet ~60% of listed requirements; women tend to apply only near 100%. Every non-essential requirement disproportionately shrinks your female applicant pool. Add a "research shows..." encouragement line (see templates) to partially offset this.

5. **Write compensation and benefits transparently.** Include a numeric salary range whenever possible: postings with ranges consistently see roughly 30-50% more applicants in platform studies, and disclosure is legally required for postings reaching candidates in California, Colorado, Washington, New York, Illinois, Hawaii, and several other US jurisdictions plus the incoming EU Pay Transparency Directive. Keep the range credible: max should be no more than ~40-50% above min (e.g., "$110,000-$150,000," not "$80,000-$200,000" — wide ranges read as evasion). State what moves a candidate within the range (experience, location tier). List 3-6 concrete benefits; "competitive benefits" is filler. If the user refuses to publish a range, warn them once about legal exposure and applicant drop-off, then comply.

6. **Run the inclusive-language pass.** Check the full draft against the swap table in Frameworks below. Specifically scan for: masculine-coded words (rockstar, ninja, dominant, aggressive, fearless — per Gaucher, Friesen & Kay's 2011 study, heavy masculine coding measurably reduces women's application rates); ableist phrasing ("must be able to stand for long periods" when the job doesn't require it, "walk the team through" is fine, "must have a driver's license" only if driving is essential); age-coded terms ("digital native," "recent grad," "high energy"); culture-fit vagueness ("work hard play hard" signals long hours and drinking culture). Target an 8th-10th grade reading level; sentences under 20 words on average; cut corporate filler ("synergize," "leverage" as a verb, "fast-paced environment" — say what it actually means: "priorities shift weekly").

7. **Add the structural essentials.** In order: title and one-line hook; 2-3 sentence role summary; outcomes/responsibilities; must-haves; nice-to-haves; compensation and benefits; about the company (3-4 sentences — pull from **brand-messaging** work if it exists); hiring process outline (number of stages and target timeline — e.g., "4 stages, ~3 weeks"; posting it lifts completion rates and reduces ghosting); EEO statement; how to apply. Total length target: **400-700 words**. Postings in this band get significantly more applications than 1,000+ word walls; under 300 words reads as low-effort.

8. **Produce channel variants.** From the master (careers-page) version, derive:
   - **LinkedIn job post:** Front-load the hook and salary in the first 200-300 characters — that's what shows before "...see more" in feeds and mobile search. Keep total under 600 words. LinkedIn's own job field truncates around 100 characters for the title.
   - **Indeed:** Bullets over paragraphs (Indeed's parser rewards structured lists), exact salary range in the dedicated field (feeds their filters — postings without it get suppressed in salary-filtered searches), plain searchable title with location.
   - **Niche boards / communities:** Cut "About us" to one sentence, lead with the most technically interesting outcome, keep under 400 words.
   - **Internal posting:** Add internal level, transfer eligibility, and the hiring manager's name.

9. **Deliver and hand off.** Present the master JD plus requested variants, list every assumption you made, and offer next steps: build the interview plan from the must-haves (**interview-kits**) and draft promotion posts (**linkedin-content**).

## Frameworks & templates

### Template 1: Master JD (fill-in-the-blank)

```
# [Searchable Job Title] — [Location / Remote policy]

**[Company] | [Full-time/Contract] | [$X–$Y salary range] | [Department]**

[One-sentence hook: the single most compelling thing about this role —
the problem, the scale, or the ownership. Max 25 words.]

## The role
[2-3 sentences: why this role exists now, who it reports to, and the
one-line mission. E.g., "Our support volume doubled in 12 months and
response times are slipping. You'll be our first dedicated Support Lead,
reporting to the COO, owning the queue and the playbook."]

## What you'll accomplish
- In your first 90 days: [concrete, verifiable milestone]
- By 6 months: [milestone]
- By 12 months: [the headline outcome, with a number]

## What you'll do
- [Verb] [what] [measurable why]   (5-8 bullets, ≤20 words each)
- ...

## What you'll need (must-haves)
- [4-7 items. Skills and evidence, not proxies. No degree requirement
  unless legally necessary.]

## Nice to have (not required — please apply without these)
- [3-5 items]

Research shows that some candidates — particularly women and people from
underrepresented groups — only apply when they meet every requirement.
If you meet most of the must-haves, we'd like to hear from you.

## Compensation & benefits
- Salary: $[X]–$[Y] [+ equity/bonus if applicable]. Placement in range
  depends on [experience / location tier].
- [3-6 concrete benefits: "$1,500/yr learning budget", "4-day onsite
  offsites twice a year", "16 weeks parental leave"]

## About [Company]
[3-4 sentences: what you do, for whom, traction, why now.]

## Hiring process
[N] stages over ~[X] weeks: [e.g., "30-min screen → take-home (≤2 hrs,
paid) → 2-hour virtual panel → offer." Name any work sample.]

[EEO statement + accommodations contact.]

**To apply:** [exact mechanism + any (short) required extras]
```

### Template 2: Worked example (condensed)

```
# Customer Success Manager — Remote (US, ET–CT hours)

Acme Analytics | Full-time | $85,000–$115,000 | Customer Team

Own the post-sale relationship for 40 mid-market accounts ($2.1M ARR)
and turn our 88% gross retention into 95%.

## The role
Our first two CSMs are at capacity and churn is concentrated in
accounts nobody owns. You'll take a defined book, report to the Head
of Customer Success, and run renewals end to end.

## What you'll accomplish
- 90 days: complete onboarding reviews with all 40 accounts; publish
  a health score for each.
- 6 months: lift NPS response rate in your book from 12% to 30%.
- 12 months: deliver 95%+ gross revenue retention on your book.

## What you'll do
- Run quarterly business reviews that surface expansion and risk early
- Own renewal negotiations up to $75K ACV without escalation
- Turn recurring support themes into product feedback the team acts on
- Build one reusable onboarding playbook per customer segment

## What you'll need
- 2+ years owning a revenue-retention number (CS, account management,
  or renewals)
- Evidence you've saved at-risk accounts — you'll walk us through one
- Comfort reading product usage data to prioritize your week
- Clear, warm written communication (most of this job is email)

## Nice to have (not required — please apply without these)
- SaaS analytics or BI-tool domain experience
- Experience with Gainsight, Vitally, or similar

[encouragement line, comp/benefits, about, 4-stage process, EEO]
```

### Requirement pruning worksheet

For each proposed requirement, ask in order; the first "no" moves it to nice-to-have or deletes it:
1. Will the hire use this in the first 90 days? (No → cut or nice-to-have)
2. Can it be learned on the job in under a month? (Yes → nice-to-have)
3. Is it a proxy? Degree → name the skill. Years → name the demonstrated capability. Tool → is it the tool or the underlying skill? ("Salesforce experience" → "experience working a pipeline in any CRM")
4. Would your best incumbent have met it at hire? (No → delete)

### Inclusive-language swap table

| Flagged | Why | Swap |
|---|---|---|
| rockstar / ninja / guru / wizard | Masculine-coded, ageist, vague | The actual title or "expert" |
| aggressive / dominant / fearless | Masculine-coded (Gaucher et al., 2011) | ambitious / decisive / takes ownership |
| work hard, play hard | Signals overwork + drinking culture | Describe real hours/culture specifics |
| fast-paced environment | Vague; often means understaffed | "Priorities shift weekly; you'll juggle 2-3 projects" |
| digital native / recent grad / young team | Age discrimination signals (ADEA risk) | "comfortable with [specific tools]" |
| culture fit | Homogeneity proxy | "values alignment: [named values]" |
| he/she throughout | Excludes; dated | "you" (second person throughout) |
| must be able to lift 50 lbs / stand all day | Only if genuinely essential | Delete unless a bona fide requirement |
| native English speaker | National-origin discrimination risk | "professional fluency in English" |
| manage stakeholders across the org | Jargon stack | "get agreement from sales, product, and finance" |

## Output format

Deliver in this order, as markdown:
1. **Assumptions & open questions** — every default applied, anything the user must confirm (especially salary range and remote policy).
2. **Master JD** — careers-page version, 400-700 words, following Template 1's structure.
3. **Channel variants** — only those requested/agreed, each labeled with its channel and length.
4. **Requirements audit table** — each must-have with a one-line justification tracing to an outcome; anything demoted to nice-to-have with the reason.
5. **Next steps** — one line each: post-and-promote suggestions, and the handoff to **interview-kits**.

## Quality checklist

Pass/fail every item before delivering:

- [ ] Title is a term candidates actually search (no internal jargon, no "ninja/rockstar"), under 60 characters
- [ ] Master JD is 400-700 words
- [ ] Contains at least three verifiable outcome statements with a number or checkable milestone
- [ ] Every responsibility bullet starts with a verb and traces to an outcome; 5-8 bullets total, each ≤20 words
- [ ] Must-haves number 4-7 and each survives the four pruning questions
- [ ] Nice-to-haves are explicitly labeled "not required" with an encouragement-to-apply line
- [ ] No degree requirement unless legally mandated for the profession
- [ ] Numeric salary range included, with max ≤ ~1.5× min, or the user explicitly declined after a legal-risk warning
- [ ] At least 3 concrete, specific benefits (no bare "competitive benefits")
- [ ] Zero hits against the inclusive-language swap table
- [ ] Written in second person ("you"), 8th-10th grade reading level
- [ ] Hiring process outlined with stage count and rough timeline
- [ ] EEO statement and accommodations contact present
- [ ] Location/remote policy and employment type stated unambiguously in the first screen of text
- [ ] LinkedIn variant front-loads hook + salary in the first 200-300 characters

## Common mistakes

- **Writing the JD before scoping the role.** If the user can't state three outcomes, you're documenting a vacancy, not defining a job. Stop and scope.
- **The Frankenstein role.** Two jobs stapled together ("full-stack engineer who also runs our paid ads") gets applicants who are mediocre at both. Split it or pick the primary function and demote the rest to nice-to-have.
- **Laundry-list requirements.** 12+ must-haves is the single most common JD failure. Each marginal requirement filters strong-but-honest candidates while confident under-qualified ones apply anyway — the filter is inverted.
- **Years-of-experience theater.** "7+ years of React" filters by biography, not capability, and invites age-related legal exposure. Requirements should name what someone can do, with what evidence.
- **Responsibilities that describe activity, not impact.** "Manage social media accounts" tells candidates nothing about scope or success. Ten vague bullets attract vague applicants.
- **Hiding compensation to preserve negotiating leverage.** It suppresses applications from exactly the candidates with options, and it's now illegal in a growing list of jurisdictions. The leverage was always imaginary.
- **A $70K-wide salary range.** Reads as either evasion or a role that's unleveled. If the range must be wide because of location tiers, say so and show the tiers.
- **Selling only, filtering never.** A JD that's all perks with no honest difficulty ("this role means being on call one week a month") generates high applicant volume and high first-year churn. Include one candid "this job is not for you if" signal.
- **Copy-pasting a competitor's JD.** Their requirements encode their team's gaps, not yours. It also imports their qualification inflation wholesale.
- **One version blasted to every channel.** The careers-page version is too long for LinkedIn's mobile preview and too unstructured for Indeed's parser. Always derive variants (Process step 8).
- **"Fast-paced, dynamic environment where you'll wear many hats."** Four clichés, zero information. Every abstraction in a JD is a question a good candidate can't get answered — and a reason they don't apply.
