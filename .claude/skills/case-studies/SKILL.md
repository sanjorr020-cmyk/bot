# Case Studies

Turn a customer's real results into a credible, reusable sales and marketing asset. A case study earns trust because it is specific: a named customer, a quantified before-state, a described implementation, and a measured after-state, told mostly in the customer's own words. This skill covers the full pipeline — picking the right customer, running an interview that produces numbers and quotes, writing the narrative, formatting it for different channels, and getting the customer's written approval — so the finished asset can survive a skeptical buyer and a legal review.

## When to use this skill

Use this skill when the user wants to:
- Write a customer case study or success story from scratch, from notes, or from an interview transcript
- Choose which customers to approach for a case study
- Prepare interview questions for a customer story call
- Extract quotes and metrics from a recorded call or transcript
- Repackage an existing case study as a one-pager, sales slide, or web page
- Set up a review/approval process with the featured customer

Do NOT use this skill for:
- Collecting short reviews, star ratings, or one-line testimonials at scale — use **reviews-and-testimonials**
- Writing the proposal that cites the case study — use **sales-proposals**
- Announcing a customer win to press — use **press-releases**
- Turning a finished case study into social posts, video, or email content — use **content-repurposing**
- Analyzing feedback themes across many customers — use **customer-feedback-analysis**
- Placing the case study on a landing page and optimizing conversion — use **landing-page-cro**

## Inputs to gather

Ask the user for these before starting. When they don't know, apply the default.

1. **Product and target buyer** — what is sold, to whom, and which segment/persona this story should persuade. Default: the company's primary ICP; check **customer-personas** output if it exists.
2. **Pipeline stage** — are we (a) selecting candidates, (b) prepping an interview, (c) drafting from a transcript/notes, or (d) formatting/approving an existing draft? Default: start at (a) and walk forward.
3. **Raw material available** — interview transcript, call recording notes, support tickets, product usage data, email threads. Default: none yet; the skill will produce the interview guide to create it.
4. **The one metric that matters** — the headline result the sales team most needs proof of (e.g., "reduces onboarding time"). Default: revenue gained, cost saved, or hours saved — in that order of preference.
5. **Formats needed** — long-form, one-pager, slide, or all three. Default: all three, drafted long-form first.
6. **Anonymity constraints** — can the customer be named? Logo usage rights? Default: assume named with logo, but flag that this must be confirmed in writing before publication.
7. **Approval requirements** — does the customer's side need legal/PR sign-off? Default: assume yes for companies >200 employees; build a 5-business-day review window into the plan.
8. **Voice and claims constraints** — from **brand-messaging** if available; any regulated-industry claim rules (health, finance). Default: plain, concrete B2B voice; no superlatives the data doesn't support.

## Process

1. **Build the candidate shortlist.** Ask the user to list 5-10 customers with good outcomes (pull from CSM notes, NPS 9-10 responders, renewal/expansion accounts, or support "thank you" threads). Score each with the Candidate Scoring Rubric below. Recommend the top 2-3; always have a backup because roughly 1 in 3 candidates declines or stalls in approval.

2. **Mine internal data first.** Before any interview, gather what the company already knows: usage stats, before/after metrics from onboarding docs, the original deal notes (why they bought, what they evaluated against). This lets interview questions confirm and sharpen numbers rather than fish for them. Decision point: if internal data already shows a quantified result, the interview's job is color and quotes; if not, the interview must establish the baseline and the delta — weight the guide accordingly.

3. **Secure participation.** Draft a short ask (5-7 sentences) from the customer's CSM or account owner, not from marketing. State the time cost (one 30-minute call), what's in it for them (exposure, a co-marketing asset, backlink, or simply the relationship), and that they get final approval over every word before anything is published. Do not promise payment; incentivized case studies must be disclosed and read as bought.

4. **Generate the interview guide.** Use the Interview Guide template below, tailored to the metric that matters. Rules: 10-14 questions for a 30-45 minute call; every question open-ended; at least 4 questions that force a number; at least 3 designed to produce a quotable emotional beat ("What would you tell someone in your old situation?"). Send 3-4 of the number-hunting questions to the customer 2-3 days ahead so they can look figures up — people rarely know their metrics cold.

5. **Conduct or process the interview.** If the user runs the call: record it (with stated consent), let silences sit, and always follow a vague answer with "Can you put a number on that?" or "Compared to what?" If the user hands you a transcript: read it twice — first pass for the story arc, second pass tagging every metric (M), every quotable line (Q), and every before-state pain (P).

6. **Extract and verify the numbers.** Build a metrics table: metric, before value, after value, timeframe, source (customer said / internal data / estimated). Decision point: any number the customer stated from memory that will appear in a headline must be confirmed in writing during approval ("You mentioned 40% — can you confirm or correct that figure?"). Never publish an estimated number as measured. If no hard number survives verification, downgrade the headline to a concrete qualitative outcome ("cut a 3-step manual process to zero") — never invent a percentage.

7. **Curate quotes.** Select 3-5 quotes per asset using the Quote Curation Rules below. Light editing for grammar and filler words is standard practice, but never change meaning, add claims, or splice sentences from different answers — every edited quote goes back to the speaker for approval as-edited.

8. **Draft the narrative.** Write long-form first using the Before/During/After arc below (800-1,500 words). Lead with the result, not the customer's founding story. Then derive the one-pager (350-500 words) and the slide (see Output format) by cutting, not rewriting — all formats must state identical numbers.

9. **Run internal review.** One pass by sales ("Would this help you close the target segment?") and one by whoever owns claims/legal. Fix before the customer sees it — customers should review once, not referee your internal edits.

10. **Run customer approval.** Send the draft with the Approval Request template below. Ask for: factual corrections, quote confirmations, written permission to use name/title/logo, and any required PR/legal sign-off on their side. Give a specific date 5 business days out. One nudge at day 3. Decision point: if the customer strips all numbers, ask whether a range ("reduced costs by 30-40%") or an anonymized version ("a mid-market logistics company") is acceptable — an anonymized story with numbers beats a named story without them.

11. **Finalize and hand off.** Log the approval email, the approved final text, and usage rights (where it may appear, expiry if any) in one place. Hand distribution to the relevant skill: **content-repurposing** for derivative assets, **sales-proposals** and **sales-call-scripts** for sales enablement, **linkedin-content** or **email-newsletters** for promotion.

## Frameworks & templates

### Candidate Scoring Rubric (score each 0-2; pursue 7+ of 10)

| Criterion | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Result strength | No measurable outcome | Qualitative win | Quantified win (%, $, hours) |
| ICP match | Wrong segment | Adjacent | Exactly who sales needs proof for |
| Name recognition | Must be anonymous | Named but unknown | Recognizable brand or title |
| Relationship health | Rocky / churn risk | Neutral | Champion who volunteers praise |
| Speed to publish | Heavy legal/PR gates | Some review | Can approve in days |

### Interview Guide template (30-45 min, adapt wording to the product)

**Warm-up (2 questions)**
1. Tell me about your role and what your team is responsible for.
2. What does a normal week look like for you?

**Before — the pain (3-4 questions; hunt the baseline)**
3. Before [product], how did you handle [problem]? Walk me through the actual steps.
4. What did that cost you — in hours, money, or missed opportunities? Rough numbers are fine.
5. What finally made you look for a solution? Was there a breaking-point moment?
6. What else did you try or evaluate, and why didn't it stick?

**During — the decision and rollout (3 questions)**
7. What almost stopped you from buying? (Surfaces objections — feed answers to **objection-handling**.)
8. How long did it take to get up and running, and what surprised you?
9. Who had to be convinced internally, and what won them over?

**After — the results (4-5 questions; force numbers)**
10. What can you do now that you couldn't before?
11. If you compare [key metric] before and after, what changed? By how much, over what period?
12. What has that meant in dollars or hours per week/month?
13. What would you tell someone who's where you were a year ago?
14. If [product] disappeared tomorrow, what would break first?

### Before/During/After narrative arc (fill in the blanks)

- **Headline:** [Customer] + [verb] + [quantified result] + [timeframe]. Example: "Northwind cut invoice processing time 62% in 90 days."
- **Results snapshot:** 3 stat callouts, immediately under the headline.
- **Before (25% of words):** Who the customer is (2-3 sentences max), the pain in concrete operational detail, the quantified cost of the status quo, the trigger event. End with a pain quote.
- **During (25%):** Why they chose you over the status quo and named/unnamed alternatives, what implementation actually involved, one honest friction point and how it was resolved (this is what makes the story believable).
- **After (40%):** Each headline metric with before → after → timeframe, one second-order benefit (morale, customer satisfaction, freed-up time reinvested), the "what's next" line showing an ongoing relationship. Anchor with the strongest quote.
- **CTA (10%):** One action matched to where this asset will live ("See how it works — book a demo").

### Quote Curation Rules

Keep a quote only if it passes all four: (1) says something a marketer couldn't say — opinion, emotion, or insider detail; (2) is specific — contains a number, a named process, or a vivid moment; (3) is 40 words or fewer (split longer ones, attribute once); (4) could not appear word-for-word in a competitor's case study. Kill on sight: "great tool, great team," "it just works," "would recommend" — those belong in **reviews-and-testimonials**, not here.

**Worked example — raw transcript to curated quote:**
- Raw: "Yeah so, I mean, honestly before this we were doing everything in spreadsheets and it was like, every Friday I'd lose basically my whole afternoon just reconciling stuff, which, you know, was not fun."
- Curated: "Every Friday I'd lose my whole afternoon reconciling spreadsheets." — Dana Okafor, Finance Lead, Northwind
- Note in approval email: "Lightly edited for length — please confirm you're comfortable with this wording."

### One-pager template (350-500 words)

```
[LOGO]  [Customer name] — [industry, size, location]

HEADLINE: [Customer] [achieved result] [timeframe]

  [STAT 1]        [STAT 2]        [STAT 3]
  62% faster      $48K saved      3 hrs/week
  invoicing       annually        recovered

THE CHALLENGE (80-120 words)
[Before-state, cost of status quo, trigger event]

THE SOLUTION (80-120 words)
[What was implemented, how long rollout took, one friction point resolved]

THE RESULTS (100-150 words)
[Each stat expanded one sentence each + second-order benefit]

> "[Strongest quote, ≤40 words]"
> — Name, Title, Company

ABOUT [YOUR COMPANY] (2 sentences) + CTA
```

### Approval Request email template

```
Subject: [Customer name] case study — your review by [date]

Hi [name],

Thank you again for the conversation on [date]. The draft case study is
attached. Before we publish anything, we need three things from you:

1. Corrections: please flag anything factually off.
2. Numbers: you mentioned [metric, e.g., "about 40% faster"] — can you
   confirm or correct that figure? We'll only publish what you confirm.
3. Permission: please reply confirming we may use your name, title, and
   [Company] logo, and note if your comms/legal team needs to review.

Nothing goes live without your written OK. Could you get this back to us
by [date, 5 business days out]? Happy to jump on a 10-minute call if
that's easier.

[CSM name]
```

## Output format

Deliver, in this order, as a single response or set of files per the user's preference:

1. **Long-form case study** — 800-1,500 words, structured: headline (≤12 words, contains the top number), 3-stat results snapshot, The Challenge / The Solution / The Results section headers, 3-5 curated quotes distributed through the body, CTA. Include a 150-160 character meta description if it will live on the web (hand deeper SEO work to **seo-content-briefs**).
2. **One-pager** — per the template above, 350-500 words, scannable in 60 seconds.
3. **Sales slide** — one slide's content as text: headline stat as the title, customer logo placeholder, 3 bullet results (each ≤10 words, each with a number), one quote ≤25 words with attribution. No paragraph text on the slide.
4. **Metrics table** — every published number with before/after/timeframe/source, for the approval email and future fact-checks.
5. **Approval package** — the filled-in approval email plus a checklist of permissions still outstanding (name, title, logo, numbers, quotes-as-edited).

If the user is earlier in the pipeline, deliver the stage-appropriate artifact instead: scored candidate shortlist with recommendation, or the tailored interview guide with the 3-4 advance questions marked "send ahead."

## Quality checklist

Pass every item before calling the asset done:

1. Headline contains a specific, customer-confirmed number (or a concrete qualitative outcome if none survived verification — never a vague "boosts productivity").
2. Every metric has before value, after value, and timeframe — a lone "300% increase" with no baseline fails.
3. Every number appears in the metrics table with a source, and headline numbers are confirmed in writing by the customer.
4. The before-state pain is operationally specific (steps, hours, dollars), not "they struggled with inefficiency."
5. At least 3 quotes pass all four Quote Curation Rules; zero generic praise quotes remain.
6. Quotes total ≤25% of the long-form word count — the narrative carries the story, quotes punctuate it.
7. The story includes one honest friction point or limitation; a frictionless story reads as fiction.
8. Customer company context is ≤3 sentences and the product is not mentioned until the "During" section.
9. All three formats state identical numbers — no drift between slide, one-pager, and long-form.
10. Written permission exists (or is explicitly tracked as pending) for name, title, logo, and each edited quote.
11. The CTA matches the asset's placement and the target buyer's stage.
12. No absolute claims the data can't support ("best," "guaranteed," "#1") and no competitor is disparaged by name.
13. Anonymized versions preserve enough context to be credible ("a 200-person logistics company in Texas," not "a company").
14. Long-form is 800-1,500 words; one-pager 350-500; slide bullets ≤10 words each.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing the vendor's story instead of the customer's.** If the subject of most sentences is your product, rewrite. The customer is the hero; the product is the tool they used.
- **Interviewing without the numbers questions pre-sent.** Ask "how much time did you save?" cold and you get "a lot." Send the quantitative questions 2-3 days ahead and you get "about 11 hours a week."
- **Publishing remembered numbers as measured ones.** "I think around 40%" becomes "40% improvement" in the headline, then the customer's finance team disputes it post-publication. Confirm in writing or publish a range.
- **The 300%-of-nothing headline.** Percentage gains off tiny baselines ("tripled signups" from 2 to 6) collapse under one skeptical question. Use absolute numbers when the baseline is small.
- **Frankenquotes.** Splicing two answers into one punchy quote. If the customer wouldn't recognize the sentence as theirs, it's fabricated attribution — a trust and sometimes legal problem.
- **Skipping the written approval because "they're friendly."** The champion leaves, their new PR head objects, and you're pulling assets from three channels. Get the email; file it.
- **Burying the result.** Opening with "Founded in 2009, Northwind is a leader in..." loses the reader before paragraph two. Result first, biography barely at all.
- **One giant asset, no derivatives.** A 1,400-word PDF nobody can paste into a proposal. Ship the slide and one-pager the same day; sales uses those 10x more.
- **Choosing the biggest logo over the best story.** A famous customer with no numbers and a 6-month legal review loses to a mid-market champion with a 62% metric and a 5-day approval.
- **Letting the customer review twice.** Sending a rough draft, then a heavily revised one, doubles their legal cycles and burns goodwill. Internal review finishes before the customer sees anything.
