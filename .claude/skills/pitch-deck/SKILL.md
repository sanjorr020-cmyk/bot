# Pitch Decks

Build and critique investor pitch decks that get meetings and survive partner-room scrutiny. This skill covers the full deck: slide-by-slide skeleton, narrative arc, the three slides that decide most outcomes (traction, market, team), the ask, appendix strategy, and adapting the same bones into a sales deck. The output is slide-by-slide content — headlines, body copy, and visual direction — not a business plan and not final visual design.

## When to use this skill

Use when the user needs to:
- Create an investor deck from scratch (pre-seed through Series B)
- Restructure or punch up an existing deck ("VCs aren't responding", "we keep losing the room at slide 6")
- Design a specific slide: traction, market size, team, ask, competition
- Build a send-ahead (email) version vs. a presented version
- Convert an investor deck into a sales deck for customer meetings

Do NOT use this skill for:
- The full written business plan or executive summary — use **business-plan**
- Building the financial model behind the projections slide — use **financial-projections** (this skill only presents its outputs)
- The market research itself (interviews, surveys, sizing data) — use **market-research**; competitor landscape research — use **competitor-analysis**
- Written sales proposals and quotes — use **sales-proposals**
- Positioning and messaging language the deck should inherit — use **brand-messaging**
- Pricing decisions shown on the business-model slide — use **pricing-strategy**

## Inputs to gather

Ask for these before drafting. Where the user doesn't know, use the stated default and flag it as an assumption.

1. **Stage and round**: pre-seed, seed, Series A/B? Default: infer from traction — no revenue = pre-seed/seed; $1M+ ARR = Series A conversation.
2. **The ask**: how much, at what stage of raising? Default: size the round to 18-24 months of runway at their projected burn.
3. **Deck context**: send-ahead (read cold in ~3 minutes) or presented live (20-30 min meeting)? Default: build the send-ahead version first — it is the gating artifact; a presented variant is a light edit.
4. **One-sentence company description**: what do you sell, to whom, why do they buy? If the user can't say it in one sentence, workshop that FIRST — it becomes the title slide.
5. **Traction numbers**: revenue/ARR, growth rate (MoM or YoY), users, retention, pipeline, LOIs, pilots. Ask for real numbers with dates. No default — a traction slide cannot be invented.
6. **Team**: founders' names, roles, and the 1-2 most credible facts about each (prior exits, domain years, relevant employers, published work).
7. **Market size inputs**: number of target customers and realistic price point (for bottom-up sizing). Default: derive from their pricing and ICP rather than accepting a quoted analyst TAM.
8. **Competition**: 3-6 named alternatives, including "spreadsheets/do nothing" if that's the real incumbent.
9. **Fundraising history**: prior rounds, amounts, notable investors. Default: omit slide if none.
10. **Business model**: how money is made, unit economics if known (CAC, LTV, gross margin, payback). Default: show pricing and gross margin only; skip CAC/LTV if pre-revenue.

## Process

1. **Nail the one-liner.** Draft 3 versions of a single sentence: "[Company] is [category] that helps [specific customer] [outcome], unlike [status quo]." Under 15 words. The user picks one. This sentence is the title-slide subtitle and the spine every slide must serve.

2. **Choose the narrative arc.** Two proven arcs — pick based on the company's strongest asset:
   - **Problem-first** (default): Problem → Solution → Why now → Market → Product → Traction → Business model → Competition → Team → Ask. Use when the problem is visceral and under-appreciated.
   - **Traction-first**: Title → Traction → Problem → Solution → ... Use when growth is exceptional (>15% MoM for 6+ months, or marquee customers). Lead with the thing that makes an investor lean in; investors decide in the first 3 slides whether to keep reading.

3. **Build the skeleton.** Lay out 10-14 slides using the template in Frameworks below. Write each slide's HEADLINE first, across the whole deck, before any body content. Read the headlines alone top to bottom — they must tell the complete story. If they don't, fix the arc before writing anything else.

4. **Write headlines as takeaways, not labels.** "Revenue grew 4x in 12 months" not "Traction". "Contractors lose 11 hours/week to manual invoicing" not "The Problem". Max ~12 words. This is the single highest-leverage edit in deck writing — investors skim headlines and look at charts; body text is tertiary.

5. **Draft slide bodies.** One idea per slide; if a slide makes two arguments, split it or cut one. Target under 40 words of body text per slide for a send-ahead deck, under 25 for a presented deck. Specify the visual for each slide (chart type, screenshot, logo wall, table) — a slide with no visual direction is unfinished.

6. **Build the traction slide with extra care** (see framework below). Choose the ONE metric that best proves the business works, chart it up-and-to-the-right over time, and annotate inflection points. Put secondary metrics in a small stat row, not competing charts.

7. **Size the market bottom-up.** (# of realistic target customers) x (achievable annual price) = SAM. Show the arithmetic on the slide. Cite a top-down analyst figure only as corroboration, never as the headline. If bottom-up SAM is under ~$1B for a venture-scale pitch, address expansion paths explicitly (adjacent segments, pricing tiers, geography) rather than inflating the base numbers.

8. **Write the ask slide.** State: amount raising, what it buys (18-24 months of runway to a named milestone), and use of funds in 3-4 buckets with percentages. The milestone must be the thing that unlocks the NEXT round (e.g., "$3M ARR and 120% NRR by Q4 2027" for a seed round targeting Series A metrics). Never put valuation on the slide.

9. **Decide appendix strategy.** Core deck stays at 10-14 slides; everything that answers a predictable diligence question goes in a 5-15 slide appendix: detailed financials, cohort retention curves, full competitive matrix, sales pipeline detail, technical architecture, regulatory analysis, cap table summary. Rule: if a slide exists to answer a question rather than advance the story, it's appendix. In live meetings, jump to appendix slides on demand — that preparedness itself signals quality.

10. **Run the two read tests.**
    - **3-minute test** (send-ahead): can a cold reader state what the company does, why now, the strongest proof point, and the ask after 3 minutes? DocSend's data puts average investor time-per-deck around 2-3 minutes; the deck must work at that speed.
    - **Headline-only test**: read only headlines; the story must be complete and compelling.

11. **Stress-test against the top objections.** Generate the 5 hardest questions a skeptical partner would ask (usually: "why won't [big incumbent] do this?", "is this a feature or a company?", "why is this team the one?", "what does the churn look like?", "why now and not 5 years ago?"). Verify each is answered in the deck or appendix; note remaining gaps to the user. For handling these live in the meeting, point the user to **objection-handling**.

12. **Produce the variant if needed.** Presented version: cut body text ~40%, enlarge visuals, min 28pt equivalent text. Sales-deck version: use the sales-deck framework below.

## Frameworks & templates

### Template 1: The 12-slide investor skeleton (each slide's single job)

| # | Slide | Single job | Headline formula |
|---|-------|-----------|------------------|
| 1 | Title | Make them want slide 2 | Company + one-liner: "[Category] for [customer] that [outcome]" |
| 2 | Problem | Make the pain visceral and quantified | "[Customer] loses [number] to [problem]" |
| 3 | Solution | Show (don't describe) the fix | "[Product] does [outcome] in [time/steps]" + product screenshot |
| 4 | Why now | Prove the timing window just opened | "[Shift] makes this possible for the first time" |
| 5 | Market | Prove the prize is big enough, bottom-up | "[# customers] x [$ price] = $[X]B reachable market" |
| 6 | Product | Demonstrate depth and defensibility | "How it works" — 3-step visual, not a feature list |
| 7 | Traction | Prove it's working (strongest slide wins) | "[Core metric] grew [X]x in [period]" |
| 8 | Business model | Show how a dollar in becomes more dollars | "[Pricing model] with [gross margin]% margins" |
| 9 | Competition | Show you know the field and win a segment | "Only [company] combines [axis 1] and [axis 2]" — 2x2 or feature table |
| 10 | Team | Prove this team is unfairly suited | "[N] founders with [domain proof]" — max 6 faces |
| 11 | Financials/roadmap | Show a credible path to the next round | "Path to [milestone] by [date]" — 3-yr summary, sourced from financial-projections |
| 12 | Ask | Name the deal | "Raising $[X]M to reach [milestone]" + use-of-funds split |

Optional slides (only if strong): Vision (slide 13, "in 10 years..."), Press/social proof, Prior investors. Cut before adding — 10 great slides beat 14 with filler.

### Template 2: Traction slide

```
HEADLINE: [Core metric] grew [X]x in [N] months
HERO VISUAL: one line/bar chart, metric vs. time, monthly granularity,
  y-axis starts at 0, 1-2 annotations ("launched self-serve", "first $50K month")
STAT ROW (3-4 small numbers under chart):
  [$XXK MRR] · [X% MoM growth] · [XX customers] · [XX% gross/net retention]
PROOF LINE: 1 sentence of qualitative proof — marquee logo, unpaid waitlist size,
  or "X% of signups from referrals"
```

Metric hierarchy — use the highest one you honestly have: revenue > usage/retention > signed contracts/pilots > waitlist/LOIs > survey interest. Benchmarks investors pattern-match against: seed-stage SaaS growing 10-15% MoM is solid, 20%+ is exceptional; Series A SaaS typically shows $1-3M ARR growing 2-3x YoY with 100%+ net revenue retention. If numbers are below benchmark, lead with the metric where you're strongest (retention, NPS, sales efficiency) rather than hiding growth in a cumulative chart.

### Template 3: Bottom-up market sizing (worked example)

```
Company: invoicing software for independent electrical contractors, $79/mo
TAM:  240,000 electrical contracting businesses in the US (Census/NAICS count)
      x $948/yr = $228M/yr — US, this product, today
SAM:  ~150,000 with 2-20 employees (our ICP) x $948 = $142M
SOM:  3% of SAM in 5 years = ~4,500 customers = $4.3M ARR
Expansion: payments processing (take rate on ~$40B invoice volume ≈ +$400M TAM),
      adjacent trades (plumbing, HVAC: 3x the business count)
```

Present it exactly like this: show the multiplication, name the source of the customer count, and put expansion logic on the slide when the base market is under ~$1B. Never present "$50B market (Gartner)" with no arithmetic — partners discount quoted TAMs to near zero, and "we only need 1% of it" is an instant credibility hit.

### Template 4: The ask + use of funds

```
HEADLINE: Raising $2.5M seed to reach $2M ARR by Q1 2028
USE OF FUNDS (pie or bar):
  55% engineering (4 hires) — ship [named product milestone]
  25% go-to-market (2 AEs + demand gen) — scale outbound motion
  15% ops/infrastructure
   5% buffer
RUNWAY: 22 months at projected burn
MILESTONES THIS BUYS: $2M ARR · 110%+ NRR · payments product live
[If applicable] Committed so far: $800K from [lead/notable angels]
```

Round-size sanity ranges (2025-2026 US norms): pre-seed $500K-$1.5M; seed $1.5-4M; Series A $8-15M. An ask far outside these ranges needs an explicit justification on the slide.

### Template 5: Sales-deck variant (customer-facing)

Same bones, different hero. Investors buy the market and the team; customers buy their own problem solved. Restructure to 8-10 slides:

1. Title — customer-outcome one-liner, not category language
2. Their problem — stated in their words, with cost quantified for a company their size
3. The shift — why the old way stopped working (replaces "why now")
4. Solution — product walkthrough mapped to THEIR workflow
5. Proof — case study with numbers ("Acme cut invoicing time 70%"; source from **case-studies**), logo wall, testimonial
6. How it works with them — implementation timeline, onboarding, support
7. Pricing — actual tiers (investor decks never show this level of detail)
8. Next step — one specific CTA: pilot scope, security review, contract start date

Cut entirely: market size, team bios, competition matrix, financials, the ask. Follow-up cadence after the meeting belongs to **follow-up-sequences**.

## Output format

Deliver a single markdown document containing:

1. **Deck summary block**: one-liner, chosen arc (problem-first or traction-first), slide count, target context (send-ahead vs. presented), stated assumptions.
2. **Slide-by-slide content** — for each slide:
   - `Slide N — [Name]`
   - **Headline:** the exact takeaway sentence
   - **Body:** exact on-slide text (bulleted, within word budget)
   - **Visual:** specific direction ("line chart, MRR by month Jan 2025-Jun 2026, annotate March launch"; "2x2: axis X = ease of setup, axis Y = depth of automation")
   - **Speaker note:** 2-3 sentences of what to SAY on this slide (presented decks only)
3. **Appendix list**: titled appendix slides with a one-line description of each and which diligence question it answers.
4. **Open gaps**: numbered list of missing data or weak points the user must resolve, each with why it matters and where to get it (pointing to sibling skills where relevant).

If the user asked for a review of an existing deck instead: score each slide against its single job (keep / rewrite / cut / move-to-appendix), run both read tests, and list the top 5 fixes in priority order.

## Quality checklist

Pass/fail every item before delivering:

1. One-liner on title slide is under 15 words and names customer + outcome.
2. Core deck is 10-14 slides; nothing that merely answers a diligence question is in the core.
3. Every headline is a takeaway sentence (contains a claim or number), not a label — zero slides titled just "Problem", "Team", "Market".
4. Headlines read in sequence tell the complete story with no gaps.
5. Each slide has exactly one idea and a specified visual.
6. Body text is under 40 words/slide (send-ahead) or 25 (presented).
7. Traction slide leads with one hero metric charted over time, y-axis at zero, with dates.
8. Market slide shows bottom-up arithmetic (count x price) with a named source for the count; no unexplained top-down TAM as the headline number.
9. Ask states amount, runway in months (18-24 target), 3-4 use-of-funds buckets summing to 100%, and a next-round-unlocking milestone with a date.
10. No valuation, no exit strategy slide, and no "1% of the market" claim anywhere.
11. Team slide has 6 or fewer people, each with 1-2 specific credibility facts (no adjective-only bios like "experienced leader").
12. Competition slide names real alternatives including the status quo; the deck never claims "no competitors".
13. Every number on every slide has an implicit or explicit source and a date; projected numbers are visually distinct from actuals.
14. The 5 hardest partner questions are each answered in core or appendix, or flagged in Open gaps.
15. Sales-deck variant (if produced) contains pricing and a single CTA, and omits market size, financials, and the ask.

## Common mistakes

- **Label headlines.** A deck of slides titled "Problem / Solution / Market / Team" forces the investor to do the synthesis. The headline row IS the pitch; everything else is evidence.
- **Leading with product instead of problem or traction.** Founders open with a 4-slide feature tour; investors haven't been given a reason to care yet. Features are slide 6, not slide 2.
- **The "1% of a $50B market" slide.** Top-down sizing with no arithmetic signals the founder hasn't found their actual buyer. Always multiply customer count by price on the slide.
- **Cumulative charts hiding flat growth.** "Total signups" always goes up and to the right. Sophisticated readers immediately ask for the monthly view — show it first or lose trust.
- **Vanity metrics as the hero.** App downloads, registered users, and press logos on the traction slide when revenue or retention exists (or is bad). Investors assume the best number you have is the one you show; showing a weak proxy implies the real numbers are worse.
- **Ask sized to a valuation target instead of a milestone.** "Raising $5M" with no stated milestone reads as "raising what we think we can get". Anchor the amount to what it buys.
- **Team slide as a LinkedIn dump.** Six bullet resumes per founder. One or two sharp facts each ("built fraud models at Stripe for 5 years", "sold DTC brand for $30M") beat any bullet list.
- **"We have no competitors."** Either the market doesn't exist or the founder hasn't looked. The status quo (spreadsheets, interns, doing nothing) is always a competitor.
- **Stuffing diligence into the core deck.** A 24-slide "complete" deck buries the story. The core sells the meeting; the appendix survives the meeting.
- **One deck for both reading and presenting.** A send-ahead deck presented live means reading walls of text at the room; a presented deck sent cold is incomprehensible. Build the send-ahead first, then strip it for the room.
- **Mixing actuals and projections in one undifferentiated line.** The moment an investor can't tell historical from hoped-for, every number in the deck becomes suspect. Use a dashed line or shaded region at the "today" boundary.
- **Skipping "why now".** Without a timing catalyst, the reflexive objection is "why hasn't Google/Salesforce done this?". Name the regulatory, technological, or behavioral shift that opened the window.
