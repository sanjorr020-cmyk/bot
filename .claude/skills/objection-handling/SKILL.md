# Objection Handling

Build a complete objection-handling playbook the user's team can run in live conversations: a catalog of their real objections classified by type, a diagnosis of what each objection actually means, a reframe using a named technique, a matched proof point, and a redirect question that moves the deal forward — plus practice scripts with realistic back-and-forth dialogue so reps can rehearse before it counts. The goal is never to "win" the argument; it is to surface the real concern, resolve it, and advance to the next step.

## When to use this skill

Use this skill when the user wants to:
- Build or improve an objection-handling playbook or battlecard for a sales team
- Craft a response to one specific objection they just received
- Prepare for a call, demo, or negotiation where they expect specific pushback
- Train reps with role-play scripts and practice dialogue
- Diagnose why deals stall at a predictable moment ("everyone goes quiet after pricing")

Do NOT use this skill for adjacent jobs — route to the sibling skill instead:
- Writing the full call structure (opener, discovery, demo, close) → **sales-call-scripts**. This skill supplies the objection-response blocks that plug into those scripts.
- Head-to-head competitive positioning research and feature comparisons → **competitor-analysis**. This skill uses its outputs to answer competitor objections; it does not produce the comparison itself.
- Deciding what to charge or how to structure discounts → **pricing-strategy**. Price *objections* are in scope; price *setting* is not.
- Re-engaging prospects who went silent → **follow-up-sequences**. Silence is not an objection.
- Responding to objections raised in a written proposal review → **sales-proposals** for the document; use this skill for the live conversation about it.
- Handling cancellation/renewal pushback from existing customers → **churn-prevention**.
- Qualifying out prospects whose "objection" is actually a disqualifier (no budget, no need, wrong fit) → **lead-qualification**.

## Inputs to gather

Ask the user for these before drafting. Where they don't know, use the stated default and label it as an assumption in the output.

1. **Product/service and price point.** What is sold, at what price, on what terms (monthly/annual, one-time, usage-based). No default — this is required.
2. **Buyer and sale context.** Who the buyer is (title, company size), average deal size, and sales cycle length. Default: assume a mid-market B2B sale, 30–90 day cycle, 2–4 stakeholders.
3. **The actual objections, verbatim.** Ask for the exact words prospects use — "too expensive" and "I can't get budget for this" are different objections. Request their top 5–10. If the user has none, generate the 8–12 most probable objections for their category across all six types and flag them as hypotheses to validate against call recordings.
4. **Current responses.** What reps say today and where it breaks down. Default: assume no documented responses exist.
5. **Proof assets on hand.** Case studies, metrics, customer logos, third-party reviews, security certifications, guarantees, pilot/trial options. Default: assume none are documented; the playbook will then specify which proof points to *create*, referencing **case-studies** and **reviews-and-testimonials** for how.
6. **Main competitor and status quo.** Who they lose to, including "do nothing" and spreadsheets/manual process. Default: status quo is the #1 competitor — it is in most B2B categories.
7. **Deliverable scope.** Full playbook, single-objection deep dive, or practice scripts only. Default: full playbook covering 8–12 objections.

## Process

1. **Collect and normalize the objection list.** Take the user's verbatim objections and rewrite each as the prospect would actually say it (first person, conversational). Merge duplicates. Target 8–12 objections for a full playbook — fewer than 6 leaves obvious gaps; more than 15 means reps won't internalize it.

2. **Classify every objection into one of six types.** Price, Timing, Authority, Competitor, Status Quo, Risk (see taxonomy in Frameworks). If the list has zero entries for any type, add the most likely objection of that type for this product — an unbalanced playbook fails on the first call that goes off-script. Decision point: if an "objection" is actually a hard disqualifier (e.g., a legal prohibition, a genuine zero-budget nonprofit for an enterprise product), mark it "qualify out" and do not write a rebuttal — route the user to **lead-qualification**.

3. **Diagnose the real meaning of each objection.** For each one, write 2–3 plausible underlying causes. "Too expensive" usually decodes to one of: (a) doesn't see the value yet, (b) comparing to a cheaper anchor, (c) has the budget but wants a discount, (d) genuinely can't afford it. The response differs for each, which is why every playbook entry must open with a clarifying question, not an answer.

4. **Write the clarifying/isolating question for each objection.** Two jobs: find out which underlying cause is in play, and isolate the objection ("If we solved X, is there anything else standing in the way?"). Isolation prevents objection whack-a-mole — resolving one concern only to have a new one appear.

5. **Write the reframe using a named technique.** Apply LAER as the default structure for every entry; use Feel-Felt-Found when social proof is the strongest asset; use the Boomerang when the objection itself can become the reason to buy (e.g., "we're too busy" → "that's exactly why"). Keep the spoken response under 30 seconds — roughly 60–80 words. Longer than that reads as defensiveness and invites interruption.

6. **Match one primary and one backup proof point to each objection.** Use the proof-matching table in Frameworks — a case study answers a risk objection well but a price objection poorly (price needs an ROI number). Every proof point must be specific: a named customer, a percentage, a timeframe. If the user has no matching asset, write the entry with a bracketed spec of the asset to create (e.g., "[Case study: customer in same industry who switched from COMPETITOR, with a before/after metric]") — this is the one permitted use of brackets in the deliverable, and each must be listed in the Proof Gaps section.

7. **Write the redirect for each entry.** Every response ends with a question that moves forward — confirming resolution ("Does that address the concern?") or advancing ("If the pilot hits those numbers, what would the approval path look like?"). A response that ends in a statement hands control back to the prospect.

8. **Draft 2–3 practice scripts.** Pick the highest-stakes objections (usually price and status quo) and write realistic dialogue: 4–6 exchanges each, where the prospect pushes back at least twice and does not fold after the first reframe. Include one script where the objection survives — reps must rehearse the graceful de-escalation to a smaller next step (pilot, reference call, revisit date) too.

9. **Add escalation rules.** Define when a rep should stop handling and start qualifying out: the same objection raised 3+ times in one call, a price objection that persists after ROI is agreed to, or an authority objection where the real buyer refuses to engage after two attempts. Deals forced past these signals close at sharply lower rates and burn pipeline time.

10. **Review against the Quality checklist**, fix failures, and deliver in the Output format below.

## Frameworks & templates

### The six objection types

| Type | Sounds like | Usually means | Best proof point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Price | "Too expensive" / "No budget" | Value not established, or anchored to a cheaper alternative | ROI math, cost-of-inaction number, payment terms |
| Timing | "Next quarter" / "Bad time" | Not a priority; no urgency; sometimes a polite brush-off | Cost-of-delay calculation, expiring incentive, quick-start plan |
| Authority | "I need to ask my boss" | Wrong stakeholder, or a soft no hiding behind a third party | Exec-ready one-pager, offer to join the internal pitch |
| Competitor | "We're looking at X" / "X is cheaper" | Comparison criteria favor the rival, or leverage for a discount | Switch story from X, head-to-head differentiator with evidence |
| Status quo | "Current setup works fine" | Change is effort; pain not quantified | Before/after metric from a similar customer, cost of the current process |
| Risk | "What if it doesn't work?" / "Security concerns" | Fear of a visible failure; personal career risk | Guarantee, pilot, certifications (SOC 2 etc.), reference call |

### LAER (default structure for every response)

- **Listen** — let the prospect finish completely; note the exact words.
- **Acknowledge** — one sentence, maximum two, validating the concern without agreeing it is a dealbreaker ("That's a fair question — most teams your size ask it").
- **Explore** — ask the clarifying question to find the underlying cause and isolate ("Help me understand — is it the total price, or the fit within this year's budget?").
- **Respond** — reframe with a matched proof point, under 30 seconds, ending in a redirect question.

The most common failure is skipping Explore: answering the stated objection instead of the real one.

### Feel-Felt-Found (use when social proof is the strongest asset)

"I understand how you **feel** — [restate concern]. [Named/described customer] **felt** the same way when [same situation]. What they **found** was [specific outcome with a number]."

Keep the "found" concrete. Cap usage at once or twice per conversation — reps who lean on it for every objection sound scripted, and experienced buyers recognize the formula.

### The Boomerang

Turn the objection into the reason to act: "You're too busy to implement new software? Teams tell us that's exactly why they buy — [customer] recovered 6 hours per rep per week within the first month." Only works when the claim is true and provable; otherwise it reads as a dodge.

### Template 1 — Playbook entry (fill in the blanks)

```
OBJECTION: "[verbatim prospect wording]"
TYPE: [Price / Timing / Authority / Competitor / Status quo / Risk]
WHAT IT USUALLY MEANS: [2–3 underlying causes, most common first]
CLARIFY & ISOLATE: "[question that reveals the cause]" +
  "If we solved [that], is anything else standing in the way?"
REFRAME (technique: [LAER / Feel-Felt-Found / Boomerang]):
  "[spoken response, 60–80 words max]"
PROOF POINT (primary): [specific asset — named customer, metric, timeframe]
PROOF POINT (backup): [second asset for when the first doesn't land]
REDIRECT: "[forward-moving question]"
DO NOT SAY: "[the tempting-but-wrong response reps default to]"
ESCALATE/QUALIFY OUT WHEN: [signal that this is not handleable]
```

### Template 1 — worked example (Price)

```
OBJECTION: "This is way more than we were planning to spend."
TYPE: Price
WHAT IT USUALLY MEANS: (1) Value not yet quantified against their numbers;
  (2) anchored to a cheaper tool that does 30% of the job; (3) discount fishing.
CLARIFY & ISOLATE: "Fair enough — when you say more than planned, is it the
  total, or how it fits this year's budget?" + "If the numbers worked out,
  is anything else holding this back?"
REFRAME (LAER): "That makes sense — nobody budgets for a tool they didn't
  know they needed. Here's the math we ran together: your team spends about
  40 hours a month on manual reporting. At your loaded cost that's roughly
  $3,800/month; we're $1,200. Even at half the time savings, you're ahead
  in month one. Should we pressure-test those hours together?"
PROOF POINT (primary): ROI worksheet built from THEIR discovery numbers.
PROOF POINT (backup): Acme Logistics case study — 11-week payback, same headcount.
REDIRECT: "If the payback holds under your numbers, what does approval look like?"
DO NOT SAY: "I can probably get you a discount." (Concedes value before
  establishing it; trains the buyer to push.)
ESCALATE/QUALIFY OUT WHEN: ROI is agreed to but "too expensive" persists —
  the real objection is elsewhere, or there is no budget authority in the room.
```

### Template 2 — Practice script (role-play dialogue)

Worked example, Status Quo objection:

```
PROSPECT: "Honestly, our current process works fine. Spreadsheets aren't
  pretty, but everyone knows them."
REP: "Totally fair — if it were falling apart you'd have replaced it already.
  Can I ask: when the quarter closes, how long does it take to get the final
  numbers everyone trusts?"
PROSPECT: "Usually about a week. Sometimes two if something doesn't reconcile."
REP: "And during those two weeks, what's on hold?"
PROSPECT: "Planning, mostly. Look, it's annoying, but it's not a fire."
REP: "That's what the ops lead at Radley said — 'annoying, not a fire.' What
  changed her mind was costing it: ten business days of stalled planning, four
  times a year, was about $60k in delayed decisions. She got close in three
  weeks with us. Would it be worth 20 minutes to run your version of that math?"
PROSPECT: "Maybe. I'd need to see it work with our data first."
REP: "Good — that's the right bar. [Objection has shifted from status quo to
  risk: switch entries.] We'd start with a two-week pilot on one team's data,
  no migration. If close time doesn't drop, you've lost two weeks. If the
  pilot works, who else would need to see the results?"
```

Note the mechanics to reproduce in every script: the prospect pushes back twice before moving, the rep asks questions before responding, numbers come from the prospect's own answers, and the script marks the moment the objection *changes type*.

## Output format

Deliver a single markdown document titled "[Company/Product] Objection Handling Playbook" containing:

1. **Coverage summary** — table of all objections: verbatim wording, type, one-line reframe strategy. One row per objection, 8–12 rows.
2. **Playbook entries** — one Template 1 card per objection, grouped by type, most frequent type first.
3. **Practice scripts** — 2–3 Template 2 dialogues for the highest-stakes objections, 4–6 exchanges each.
4. **Escalation & qualify-out rules** — the 3–5 signals that mean stop handling.
5. **Proof gaps** — bulleted list of proof assets referenced but not yet existing, each with a one-line spec (cross-reference **case-studies** or **reviews-and-testimonials** where relevant).
6. **Assumptions** — every default the user didn't confirm.

For a single-objection request, deliver one Template 1 card plus one practice script — skip the rest.

## Quality checklist

Verify every item before delivering; fix failures rather than shipping them.

- [ ] Every objection is written verbatim in first-person prospect language, not paraphrased category labels ("It's too expensive," not "Price concerns").
- [ ] All six types are represented, or the absence of a type is explicitly justified.
- [ ] Every entry opens with a clarifying question — no entry jumps straight to a rebuttal.
- [ ] Every entry includes an isolating question ("anything else standing in the way?").
- [ ] Every spoken reframe is 80 words or fewer (test: read aloud in under 30 seconds).
- [ ] Every acknowledgment is one to two sentences — no groveling, no "I completely understand your frustration" padding.
- [ ] Every entry has a primary and backup proof point, each with a specific name, number, or timeframe — zero generic claims like "many customers see great results."
- [ ] Proof point type matches objection type per the matching table (e.g., risk objections get a guarantee/pilot/reference, not a feature list).
- [ ] Every entry ends with a redirect question, not a statement.
- [ ] Every entry includes a "DO NOT SAY" line naming the common wrong response.
- [ ] Practice scripts have 4–6 exchanges with at least two prospect pushbacks — no scripts where the prospect folds after one reframe.
- [ ] At least one script shows the objection changing type mid-conversation and the rep adapting.
- [ ] Escalation/qualify-out rules exist with concrete triggers (counts, conditions), not vibes.
- [ ] Missing proof assets appear in the Proof Gaps section; brackets appear nowhere else in the document.
- [ ] Nothing in the playbook asks the rep to make a claim the user hasn't confirmed is true.

## Common mistakes

- **Answering the stated objection instead of the real one.** "Too expensive" gets an instant discount offer when the prospect actually meant "I don't see the value." Diagnosis before response — every entry, no exceptions.
- **Rebutting brush-offs as if they were objections.** "Send me some info" and "call me next quarter" are usually polite exits, not concerns to overcome. The right move is a qualifying question ("Happy to — so I send the right thing, what would need to be in it for this to be worth a follow-up call?"), not a rebuttal.
- **Objection whack-a-mole.** Resolving concerns one at a time without isolating lets the prospect generate an infinite queue. Isolate before investing in a full response.
- **Over-acknowledging.** Three sentences of empathy before the answer reads as agreement that the objection is fatal. One sentence, two at most, then explore.
- **Trash-talking the competitor.** Answering "we're looking at X" by attacking X makes the prospect defend their shortlist. Praise the choice of category, then differentiate on the one or two criteria where the evidence is strongest.
- **Feel-Felt-Found on every objection.** Used more than twice in a conversation, the formula becomes audible and credibility drops. It is a social-proof tool for risk and status-quo moments, not a universal wrench.
- **Proof points without numbers.** "Lots of companies like yours love us" handles nothing. Every proof point needs a name (or precise description), a metric, and a timeframe — or it goes in Proof Gaps until it exists.
- **Writing responses reps can't say aloud.** 150-word paragraphs with three sub-clauses die in a live call. If it can't be spoken naturally in under 30 seconds, cut it.
- **Treating every objection as handleable.** Some objections are the buyer telling the truth: no budget, no authority, no need. A playbook without qualify-out rules turns reps into deal zombies chasing closed-lost pipeline.
- **Discounting as the default price response.** Conceding price before re-establishing value trains buyers to object and permanently damages deal economics. Price moves only after value is agreed and only in exchange for something (longer term, case-study rights, faster signature) — the mechanics of that trade belong to **pricing-strategy**.
